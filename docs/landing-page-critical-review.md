# Landing Page Design: Critical Review Standards

A landing page exists to move a stranger from arrival to a decision. Everything else is decoration until it serves that job. Review against the sections below. Fail a section when the page cannot defend its choices under scrutiny.

---

## 1. AIDA Pipeline

**Attention → Interest → Desire → Action** is not a slogan. It is the vertical spine of the page. If a visitor cannot name which stage they are in after five seconds of scroll, the pipeline is broken.

| Stage | Job | What to look for | Fail when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | Stop the scan | First viewport owns one dominant claim, one brand signal, one visual anchor | Hero competes with itself: three headlines, floating badges, split panels, or a title that could sit on any SaaS site |
| **Interest** | Make them care | Problem or mechanism appears next, concrete and product-specific | Feature lists arrive before the visitor understands what the product *is* |
| **Desire** | Make them want it | Proof, consequence, or lived outcome; social or institutional credibility | Claims without evidence; testimonials that read like copy, not people |
| **Action** | Close the loop | One primary CTA, repeated at decision points without nagging | Multiple equal CTAs; CTA buried; CTA that changes meaning mid-page |

**Reviewer’s test:** Cover the nav. Read only the section headlines top to bottom. Do they tell a complete AIDA story? If not, the page is a collage, not a funnel.

Secondary CTAs are allowed only when they serve a different readiness level (e.g. “See how it works” vs “Start trial”). They must never visually equal the primary action.

---

## 2. Look and Feel

Look and feel is the page’s institutional temperature: how serious, how playful, how expensive, how trustworthy. Visitors decide this before they finish the headline.

Ask:

- Does the surface feel like a product category peers would respect, or like a pitch deck someone animated?
- Is the atmosphere built from materials (paper, stone, soft light, product photography) or from abstract gradients and stock metaphor?
- Would removing the logo make this page interchangeable with a competitor’s?

**Critical stance:** “Premium” is not more shadow, more blur, or more glass. Premium is restraint with intention: fewer surfaces, clearer hierarchy, materials that look like they belong in the same room. Playfulness is earned through one controlled gesture (a highlight, a motion beat, a type choice), not through stickers, clay borders, and emoji punctuation.

---

## 3. Colour Balance

Colour is hierarchy with a palette. Most landing pages fail by treating accent colour as decoration instead of as a scarce signal.

**Rules of thumb for review:**

1. **One dominant field.** The page needs a base (usually light institutional, dark editorial, or warm paper). Flat single-fill is allowed only if atmosphere comes from texture, type, or imagery instead.
2. **One accent for action.** CTA colour should appear almost nowhere else. If ice-blue (or brand accent) shows up on icons, underlines, chips, *and* buttons, the button stops being special.
3. **Neutrals do the work.** Body text, rules, captions, and secondary UI live in a narrow grey/navy band. High-chroma greys and rainbow icons are noise.
4. **Contrast is non-negotiable.** Text on field must pass readable contrast in the actual rendered state (including over images and gradients). Pretty-but-illegible fails.
5. **Brand colours are not a free-for-all.** A navy + ice system means navy carries weight and ice carries highlight. Introducing a third saturated hue requires a written reason.

**Balance check:** Squint until the page is blobs. You should still see: a calm field, one dark mass (type or CTA), one accent spark, and one image mass. Four competing colour masses means the palette is unmanaged.

---

## 4. Borders, Edges, and Visual Consistency

Borders are grammar. They tell the eye what is a container, what is a divider, and what is chrome.

**Match the rest of the system:**

| System character | Border behaviour |
|---|---|
| Corporate / institutional | Hairlines, low-contrast rules, almost no radius, or soft large radius used sparingly |
| Product UI embedded in marketing | Match the product’s real control chrome; do not invent a second UI language |
| Editorial / fashion | Often *no* card borders; separation by type, whitespace, and full-bleed media |
| Playful / toy | Thick ink borders only if *every* interactive surface agrees; mixed thin/thick is chaos |

**Fail conditions:**

- Cards with borders *and* shadows *and* fills when one device would suffice
- Rounded cards next to sharp photography next to pill chips with no shared radius scale
- Decorative frames around the hero image that fight the brand’s actual product UI
- Dividers that are thicker or higher-contrast than the primary CTA

**Reviewer’s test:** Delete every border on a section. If hierarchy collapses, the layout was depending on lines instead of structure. If hierarchy holds, the borders were costume.

---

## 5. Typography and Impression

Type is the loudest non-image signal of seriousness.

**What type implies (review against brand intent):**

- Default stacks (Inter, Roboto, Arial, system UI) read as unfinished or template unless the brand is deliberately invisible
- Soft rounded display faces read playful / consumer / juvenile; dangerous next to academic or compliance claims
- High-contrast serifs and fashion display faces read editorial and expensive; they fail when paired with toy UI chrome
- Geometric sans at restrained sizes reads institutional SaaS; at oversized weights with tight tracking it reads startup-aggressive

**Hierarchy requirements:**

1. One display voice for the promise (headline)
2. One text voice for reading (lede, body)
3. Optional mono or secondary for metadata / indices / UI chrome
4. Cap heights and line lengths that can be scanned: headlines short enough to land in one or two lines at desktop; body ~60–75 characters where continuous reading matters

**Impression check:** Change only the typefaces to Times + Arial. If the page suddenly feels more trustworthy, the current display face was fighting the claim. If it feels worse, the display face was doing real brand work and should stay.

Tracking, weight, and size must agree with the AIDA stage: Attention can be large and sparse; Interest and Desire need readable text, not poster type.

---

## 6. Branding

Brand is not the logo in the corner. Brand is whether the first viewport could belong to anyone else after the nav is removed.

**Must hold:**

- Company name or mark at hero-level presence (wordmark scale, placement, or integration into the composition)
- Colour, type, and imagery that match how the company wants to be remembered in its category
- Product truth: what you sell appears as itself, not as abstract illustration of “innovation”

**Fails:**

- Generic headline that works for five competitors with a logo pasted on
- Brand mark smaller than the legal footer
- Visual world borrowed from a trendy reference (clay, glassmorphism, broadsheet) that contradicts category trust needs
- Inconsistent naming (“Bloombal” vs product category vs feature nicknames) without a clear hierarchy

**Critical stance:** Branding that only appears in the navbar is branding as afterthought. The first composition should fail the “swap the logo” test: a competitor’s mark should look wrong on this page.

---

## 7. Simplicity / Complexity Balance

Simple is not empty. Complex is not rich. The question is *cognitive load per scroll beat*.

**Aim:**

- **One job per section.** One headline, one supporting sentence, one primary visual or proof block
- **Hero budget.** First viewport: brand, one headline, one short lede, one CTA group, one dominant image. No stats strips, logo clouds, schedule cards, or secondary promos in the opening frame
- **Progressive disclosure.** Detail deepens as commitment deepens; the first screen persuades, later screens prove

**Where complexity earns its place:**

- Product UI shown for real (legible, not a tiny fake screenshot)
- Comparison or process only when the category is unfamiliar
- Dense proof for sceptical B2B buyers *after* Desire has started

**Fail when:**

- Every section is a card grid of equal weight
- Icons explain what sentences already said
- Motion, texture, and copy all compete at once
- “Simple” means sparse white void with nothing to hold onto; “complex” means dashboard cosplay on a marketing page

**Reviewer’s test:** Remove one element from each section. If nothing is lost, it should already be gone.

---

## 8. Placement, Reading Order, and Scan Paths

In Latin-script markets, people scan top → bottom and left → right, with F-shaped and Z-shaped patterns on marketing pages. Layout must respect that, not fight it for novelty.

**First viewport composition:**

1. Brand / nav (top recognition)
2. Headline in the primary optical centre (not below a fold of chrome)
3. Lede immediately under the claim
4. CTA in the continuation path of the eye (usually under lede or at the end of the Z)
5. Dominant visual either full-bleed behind / beneath, or as the mass that completes the Z, never as a competing second headline

**Down-page:**

- Section entry: headline first, then support, then evidence
- Avoid left-column walls of text with unrelated right-column widgets that restart the story
- Sticky CTAs and nav must not cover the reading line of primary content
- Proof and logos belong where Desire lives, not where Attention should still be forming

**RTL and localization note:** If the product ships in RTL languages, mirroring is a product requirement; for a single-market English page, optimize for LTR scan without pretending universality.

**Fail when:** Important claims sit in the bottom-right of a busy hero; CTAs float as orphans; columns force the eye to zigzag without a narrative reason.

---

## 9. Texture of the Page

Texture is what keeps a page from feeling like a slide: paper grain, photographic atmosphere, soft depth, hairline rules, material photography, quiet noise. Flat fills with hard UI cards stack into “deck mode.”

**Good texture:**

- Comes from real materials or restrained digital equivalents (paper field, soft vignette, product surface, natural light)
- Varies by section without changing brand world mid-scroll
- Supports type readability instead of fighting it

**Bad texture:**

- Random gradients that do not belong to a material story
- Heavy grain over small type
- Every section a new background colour with no bridge
- Fake depth (glow stacks, neon edges) on an otherwise corporate claim set

**Critical stance:** A page can be mostly white and still have texture if type, spacing, photography, and one material plate do the atmospheric work. A page can be full of images and still feel flat if every image is a rounded card on the same cream fill.

## 10. Animation Standards

Motion is presence and hierarchy, not entertainment. If a visitor remembers the animation and forgets the offer, motion failed.

### Purpose

Ship **2–3 intentional motions** on a visually led landing page, each with a job:

1. **Entrance / presence** — first viewport arrives with controlled opacity/transform so the page does not “pop” lifelessly
2. **Scroll hierarchy** — one scrubbed or triggered reveal that marks a section change (clip, pin, parallax of a *meaningful* layer)
3. **Feedback** — CTA hover/focus, form state, or demo playback control

Additional motion must justify itself or be cut.

### Craft rules

| Rule | Standard |
|---|---|
| Duration | UI feedback 150–250ms; section entrance 400–800ms; long cinematic scrub only when scroll-driven and interruptible |
| Easing | Prefer deceleration for entrances; avoid bounce/elastic on corporate surfaces unless brand is explicitly playful |
| Distance | Small travel (8–24px) for text; large travel only for staged media |
| Once vs loop | Marketing content animates once into place; infinite loops are reserved for product demos that show real behaviour |
| Scroll | Prefer scrubbed ScrollTrigger-style motion tied to scroll position over surprise timed animations mid-read |
| Reduced motion | Honour `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`: replace parallax/scrub with static states; keep essential opacity if needed for comprehension |

### What to ban

- Fade-up on every card in sequence (PowerPoint build)
- Staggered icon rows that delay comprehension
- Autoplaying motion that competes with reading
- Parallax on body text
- Hover effects that shift layout (reflow jitter)
- Loading theatres longer than the content’s value
- Scroll-jacking that steals control for more than a short pinned beat

### Integration with the rest of the system

Animation must inherit the page’s temperature:

- Institutional pages: quiet opacity, soft y-translate, pin/reveal of product media
- Fashion / editorial: decisive clip-paths, typographic reveals, still photography with restrained motion
- Playful brands: may use spring or character motion, but still limited count and clear hierarchy

**Reviewer’s tests:**

1. Disable all animation. Does the page still make sense and still look finished? If not, motion was hiding weak layout.
2. Watch once without scrolling, then scroll quickly. Does anything feel sticky, late, or nauseating?
3. Count distinct motion “ideas.” More than three without a demo requirement is usually indulgence.

## Quick Failure Checklist

Use at the end of any review. One “yes” is a material issue; three is a redesign trigger.

- [ ] First viewport fails AIDA Attention (no single claim + brand + visual)
- [ ] Accent colour appears more often than the primary CTA
- [ ] Borders/radius/shadow language disagrees with type and imagery
- [ ] Typeface impression fights category trust
- [ ] Logo could be swapped with a competitor’s without dissonance
- [ ] Sections share equal visual weight (no hierarchy)
- [ ] Eye path does not follow claim → support → action
- [ ] Page feels like slides: flat fills, card stacks, build animations
- [ ] Motion is decorative, ignores reduced-motion, or exceeds 2–3 purposeful beats
- [ ] Texture is either absent (deck) or chaotic (new world every section)

---

## How to Write the Review

1. State the page’s intended job and audience in one sentence.
2. Walk AIDA top to bottom with evidence (screenshots or section names).
3. Score look, colour, borders, type, brand, complexity, placement, texture, motion against the standards above.
4. Separate **blocking** issues (hurts conversion or trust) from **polish** (craft debt).
5. Prescribe the smallest change that restores hierarchy; do not recommend a new aesthetic unless the current world cannot carry the claim.

The standard is not “beautiful.” The standard is: a specific stranger understands the offer, trusts the company, and knows what to do next, without the page apologizing through clutter or performing through motion.
